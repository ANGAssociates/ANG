3 Things Pharma Leaders Should Get Right on AI in 2026

ANG Associates — Life Sciences & AI Consulting · Aug 2026 · 8 min read

Most pharma companies do not have an AI ambition problem. They have a scaling problem. Deloitte's 2026 Life Sciences Outlook, based on a survey of 280 biopharma and medtech C-suite executives, found that 78% expect AI to play a central role in driving major change this year, yet only 22% say they have actually scaled AI successfully, and just 9% report significant returns on what they have spent so far.1 That gap, between what leaders say about AI and what their organizations have actually delivered, is the real story for 2026. Closing it comes down to three things.

The AI ambition-to-value gap, in numbers

  • 70% of pharma leaders call AI an "immediate priority," rising to 85% among top-ranking Big Pharma companies.2
  • Only 40% of AI pilots at large pharma and biotech companies advance from pilot to scaled deployment.3
  • 17% of tech leaders see measurable payoff from AI in research and discovery today; 29% in clinical development.4
  • Over 80% of surveyed pharma companies are increasing AI budgets, while only 15% hold budgets flat.2

Sources: Deloitte 2026 Life Sciences Outlook; Define Ventures / Fierce Pharma; ZS 2026 CDIO Research; ZS Pharma Industry Outlook 2026. Full citations below.

Budgets are not the constraint. What separates the small group of companies pulling ahead from the majority still stuck in pilot purgatory is how they handle three things: data and objective-setting, risk-tiered governance, and the operating model around AI. Here is what the evidence says pharma leaders should actually do about each.

1. Fix data quality and objective-setting before you scale anything

ZS's 2026 CDIO research, a Harris Poll survey of 115 senior technology executives at large pharma and biotech companies conducted in July 2025, asked leaders why AI initiatives stall before reaching scale. The top three reasons were not technical sophistication or model performance. They were weak data quality and governance (cited by 68%), unclear objectives and success metrics (67%), and lack of clear business ownership (63%).3

This matters because it reframes where leadership attention should go. Many pharma organizations are still treating AI as a tooling decision: which model, which vendor, which platform. The executives ZS surveyed are increasingly treating it as a data and accountability decision instead. One surveyed CIO described the shift bluntly: "We've learned enough with AI to move past experimentation," now concentrating on five to ten high-impact use cases with a defined 20-30% ROI target rather than a wide portfolio of pilots.3

In practice, this means before funding another AI pilot, pharma leaders should be able to answer three questions in writing: what data does this use case require, and is it AI-ready today; who in the business owns the outcome, not just IT; and what specific metric defines success before the pilot starts. ZS found that 67% of respondents flagged the absence of that third answer as a critical mistake.3 It is a cheap question to ask and an expensive one to skip.

2. Build risk-tiered AI governance, aligned to where regulation is actually heading

Pharma is one of the few industries where AI governance is not optional; it intersects directly with GxP validation, patient safety, and regulatory submissions. The good news is that the regulatory picture became considerably clearer in the past twelve months. FDA's Center for Drug Evaluation and Research published draft guidance in January 2025, "Considerations for the Use of Artificial Intelligence to Support Regulatory Decision Making for Drug and Biological Products," setting out a risk-based framework for AI used to generate evidence supporting safety, effectiveness, or quality claims.5

That was followed, on 14 January 2026, by a joint FDA-EMA document, "Guiding Principles of Good AI Practice in Drug Development," setting out ten shared principles spanning the medicine lifecycle from early research and clinical trials through manufacturing and safety monitoring.6 European Commissioner for Health and Animal Welfare Olivér Várhelyi described it as "a first step of a renewed EU-US cooperation" on AI in medicine.6 For pharma leaders, this is the first time US and EU regulators have converged on a shared reference point, which means governance frameworks can now be designed once rather than duplicated per jurisdiction.

The mistake to avoid is applying one governance standard to everything. ZS's research points to risk stratification as the differentiator between companies that govern well and companies that govern slowly: GxP-critical processes, such as adverse event detection or data supporting a regulatory submission, warrant strict, validated controls, while lower-risk internal use, such as forecasting or first-draft documentation, does not need the same review cycle.3 Separately, Define Ventures' study of C-suite executives at 16 of the top 20 pharma companies found 80% already have a dedicated AI governance structure in place, with most of those structures focused specifically on ethics and safety.2 The leaders ahead of the curve are not the ones with the strictest governance; they are the ones whose governance intensity matches the actual risk of the use case.

3. Redesign the operating model, including a deliberate build, buy, and partner decision

ZS's research found that the capabilities under the most pressure to change are not primarily technical. Executives ranked business decision-making processes (56%) and talent and skills (58%) alongside technology capability (61%) as needing to evolve to close the scaling gap.3 Roughly 86% of surveyed companies are already testing or implementing new roles and team structures to support AI, and 55% of CIOs say they already hold the authority to reshape enterprise operating models to support it.3 AI scaling in pharma is, at this point, as much an org-design problem as an engineering one.

Part of that redesign is a clear-eyed build, buy, and partner decision, made deliberately rather than by default. Define Ventures found pharma companies splitting roughly into thirds: 40% pursuing a hybrid internal-and-external model, 30% prioritizing in-house tools, and 30% going external-first.2 ZS found 61% of pharma tech leaders specifically planning external AI partnerships going forward, generally to bring in delivery experience and domain expertise rather than to hand off strategy.3 The pattern across both studies is consistent: companies are not trying to build everything themselves, and they are not trying to buy their way out of the hard parts either. They are building internal ownership of governance, data, and business outcomes, while partnering for the parts of execution, workflow redesign, validation, change management, that move faster with outside experience.

Where to start this quarter

  • Pick 5-10 AI use cases with a named business owner and a written success metric, not a portfolio of unowned pilots.
  • Map your current and planned AI use cases against a simple risk tier: GxP-critical versus lower-risk, and check governance intensity against each tier rather than applying one policy to all.
  • Read the FDA-EMA "Guiding Principles of Good AI Practice in Drug Development" (January 2026) against your current AI governance framework and note the gaps.6
  • Decide, function by function, what you will build, what you will buy, and where a delivery partner closes the gap faster than either option alone.
